Abstract
Assigning class labels to multivariate time series simultaneous recordings from multiple sensors
arises in cardiac diagnostics, seizure monitoring, industrial fault detection, gesture recognition,
and traffic-flow analysis. The difficulty is not merely scale: three qualitatively distinct dependency
structures must be resolved at once. Within each channel, relevant events unfold through time.
Meaningful patterns frequently arise from the interactions between various channels rather than
from any one channel alone. As the recording progresses, these inter-channel interactions may
also change, making the underlying dynamics extremely non-stationary. Few strategies are able
to address all three issues in a single framework, however existing methods usually succeed in
addressing some parts of this issue.
This thesis proposes theSpatiotemporal Graph-of-Graphs (ST-GoG) Framework. A
Residual TCN with Squeeze-and-Excitation attentionextracts per-channel temporal
features through five dilated causal convolutional blocks at dilation factors d∈ { 1, 2, 4, 8, 16};
an SE block then reassigns importance weights across feature maps based on their aggregate
discriminative signal. AMulti-Scale GCNprocesses the TCN embeddings through three
independent graph convolutional branches, each using chain-adjacency at a different radius
(r∈ { 1, 3, 5}), with learnable softmax scalars controlling how the three outputs are blended. A
hierarchical GoG modulepartitions each recording into temporal segments, applies an inner
GCN within each segment, and uses an outer GCN to propagate context across segments.
Training uses MixUp augmentation ( α = 0.2), cosine annealing with warm restarts ( T0=50,
Tmult=2), exponentially decaying stochastic depth, adaptive label smoothing, and seven-pass
test-time augmentation. A learnable four-pathway softmax head combines all four feature
streams before classification.
Evaluated on all 22 UEA datasets, ST-GoG achieves the highest mean test accuracy (0.743)
and wins on more individual datasets than any single competing method. Gains are sharpest on
small-training-set medical benchmarks and structured motion-capture tasks.
Keywords –Multivariate Time Series Classification, Graph Convolutional Networks, Temporal
Convolutional Networks, Graph-of-Graphs, Spatiotemporal Learning, Hierarchical Pooling,
Squeeze-and-Excitation, MixUp, Deep Learning.

Introduction
Sensor networks, clinical instruments, and motion-capture systems all produce the same kind of
raw output: a table of numbers in which each column records a measurement channel and each
row corresponds to a moment in time. The scientific value of that table lies not in any individual
reading but in the patterns that emerge across consecutive rows the periodic oscillation that
characterises a healthy heartbeat, the transient amplitude spike that precedes a mechanical
failure, the co-ordinated sweep of joint angles that distinguishes one gesture from another.
Assigning a class label to such a recorded sequence deciding, from the pattern of measurements
alone, which of several known categories the recording belongs to is the task of time series
classification (TSC). It arises in nearly every domain that relies on continuous instrumentation,
from healthcare and structural monitoring to robotics and activity recognition.
Considerable progress has been made on the univariate version of this problem, where a single
channel is observed. The harder and less-solved variant arises when several channels are recorded
simultaneously the setting known asMultivariate Time Series Classification (MTSC). In
the multivariate case the model must jointly address three interdependent challenges: detecting
discriminative patternswithinindividual channels, characterising the structural dependency
betweenchannels, and tracking how that inter-channel dependency changes as the recording
progresses. A model that handles only one or two of these challenges ignores part of the data
that may most clearly distinguish the classes from one another. This combined temporal-and-
structural problem is the central subject of this thesis.

1.1 Classical Approaches
The earliest successful TSC methods were distance-based. The1-Nearest Neighbour classifier
paired with Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)[11] became the dominant baseline for over
a decade. DTW is an elastic alignment measure that finds the optimal non-linear mapping
between two sequences, allowing comparison even when they differ in speed or phase. Formally,
given two sequencesa= (a 1, . . . , am) andb= (b 1, . . . , bn), DTW computes:
DTW(a,b) = min
W
X
(i,j)∈W
d(ai, bj),
where W is a warping path a sequence of index pairs ( i, j) satisfying boundary, monotonicity,
and continuity conditions. Despite its effectiveness on small-to-medium datasets, DTW scales as
O(mT) per pair, making it infeasible for large datasets or real-time applications.
Feature-based methods offered an alternative: extract a fixed-dimensional feature vector
from each time series and train any off-the-shelf classifier. Common features include statistical
moments (mean, variance, skewness), frequency-domain descriptors (FFT coefficients, spectral
entropy), and symbolic representations (SAX [20], shapelets [19]). TheROCKET[6] framework
pushed this paradigm to its extreme efficiency: a large bank of random convolutional kernels of
varying lengths and dilations is applied to each series, and the maximum value and proportion
of positive values of each convolution output are extracted as features. A simple linear classifier
(ridge regression) trained on these features achieves near state-of-the-art accuracy at a fraction
of the computational cost of ensemble or deep learning methods.
1.2 Deep Learning for TSC
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) were among the first deep architectures applied suc-
cessfully to TSC. AFully Convolutional Network (FCN)[15] consisting of stacked 1D
convolution, batch normalization, and ReLU layers with a Global Average Pooling (GAP)
head achieved competitive accuracy without any handcrafted features.ResNet[17] variants
introduced skip connections to enable deeper architectures.InceptionTime[8], inspired by
Google’s Inception module, applied multiple convolutional filters of different kernel sizes in
parallel and fused their outputs, capturing patterns at multiple temporal scales simultaneously.
Recurrent architectures, particularlyLong Short-Term Memory (LSTM)networks and
their variants, were another important direction. An LSTM maintains a hidden state that
evolves over time, allowing it to capture long-range dependencies. TheLSTM-FCNmodel [16]
combined an LSTM branch with a parallel FCN branch and concatenated their outputs before
classification, achieving strong results across diverse datasets. However, LSTMs suffer from
vanishing gradients over very long sequences, and their sequential nature prevents efficient
parallel computation on modern hardware.
Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs)[1] were proposed as a superior alternative
to recurrent networks for sequential modeling. TCNs use 1D dilated causal convolutions stacked
in residual blocks. Dilation exponentially increases the receptive field of each layer without
increasing the number of parameters, enabling TCNs to capture patterns at arbitrarily long

Transformer-basedarchitectures [13] applied self-attention mechanisms to TSC, computing

1.3 Multivariate Time Series Classification (MTSC)
Definition 1.1(Multivariate Time Series).A multivariate time series is a matrix of observations
X∈R C×T , where C is the number of channels (also called variables, sensors, or dimensions)
and T is the temporal length. The c-th row Xc,: ∈R T is the univariate sub-series for channel c.
Definition 1.2(Multivariate Time Series Classification).Given D = {(X (i), y(i))}N
i=1 with
X (i) ∈R C×T , MTSC learns f : RC×T → Y minimizing prediction error over the joint space of
temporal and cross-channel patterns.
MTSC is strictly harder than univariate TSC because a complete model must address three
tightly coupled dependency structures simultaneously. The first concerns intra-channel temporal
patterns periodicities, trends, and transient events which are the same structures that univariate
TSC methods target. The second concerns inter-channel spatial relationships: correlations and
causal influences between distinct measurement channels at the same or nearby time steps. The
third, and most frequently neglected, concerns the temporal evolution of those inter-channel
correlations; in many real-world signals the coupling between sensors is itself non-stationary, so
a static spatial graph cannot fully represent the data.
1.3.1 Graph-Based Approaches to MTSC
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), and specifically Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs), have
emerged as a natural framework for capturing inter-channel relationships [18]. In a channel
graph, each node represents one channel (sensor or variable), and edges encode the pairwise
similarity or causal influence between channels.
1.4 Motivation for the Proposed Work
Despite the progress surveyed above, a closer look at published MTSC systems reveals a recurring
pattern: temporal encoding and graph-based relational reasoning are designed independently
and merged only at the very end, usually through fixed-weight concatenation. This matters
because the two kinds of information are not equally useful on every dataset on a three-channel
EEG recording, inter-channel structure may dominate; on a 963-sensor traffic network, temporal
periodicity may be the more reliable cue. Hardwiring an equal blend ignores this variability.
Beyond fusion strategy, three other gaps are worth noting. Graph convolutional layers
in existing methods almost always operate at a single, pre-fixed neighbourhood radius, so
relationships at other spatial scales are simply invisible to the model. SAGoG’s pairwise inter-
instance graph, while effective, scales as O(N 2T 2) per training step workable on the smaller
UEA benchmarks but prohibitive as dataset size grows. And the adjacency itself is typically
3

--- PAGE 16 ---
static within a forward pass, preventing the model from reflecting the non-stationary coupling
patterns that appear in real physiological and mechanical signals.
TheST-GoG Frameworkproposed here is structured as a direct response to each of these
four limitations.
1.5 Contributions of This Work
This work makes four principal contributions. First, it introduces abaseline spatiotemporal
architecture(TCN–GCN–GoG) that cleanly integrates dilated temporal encoding, single-
scale graph convolution, and a lightweight Graph-of-Graphs readout within one end-to-end
trainable pipeline, establishing a reproducible starting point for ablation. Second, it develops
theimproved ST-GoG architecture, which augments the baseline with residual TCN
blocks enhanced by Squeeze-and-Excitation channel attention, a multi-scale GCN operating
simultaneously at three chain-adjacency radii ( r∈ { 1, 3, 5}) with learnable softmax fusion,
an adaptive hierarchical GoG whose segment count is determined by sequence length, and a

2.1 Overview
Research in time series classification has followed two broad trajectories. The first trajectory
feature-based methodsfocuses on extracting increasingly powerful representations from raw
sequences, culminating in random kernel transforms and deep convolutional architectures. The
second trajectorystructure-based methodsemphasizes explicitly modeling the relational structure
between variables or instances, culminating in graph neural network approaches. The proposed
ST-GoG framework occupies the intersection of these two trajectories.
2.2 Distance-Based and Feature-Based Methods
2.2.1 Dynamic Time Warping and Elastic Measures
Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) [11] remains one of the most reliable baselines for univariate TSC.
Its key insight is that meaningful temporal patterns can be shifted, compressed, or expanded in
time, and the DTW alignment finds the minimum-cost elastic mapping between two sequences.
Extensions to multivariate data include independent-channel DTW (I-DTW) and dependent
DTW (D-DTW) [22]. All elastic measures share the fundamental O(T 2) per-pair cost, which
limits applicability to large datasets.
2.2.2 Interval and Shapelet Methods
Shapelets [19] are subsequences whose presence or absence is maximally discriminative for
class membership. TheBOSS(Bag of Symbolic Fourier Approximation Symbols) method [21]
and its multivariate extensionWEASEL+MUSE[5] use Fourier coefficients and symbolic
approximation to extract discriminative frequency-domain features efficiently.
2.2.3 Random Kernel Transforms
ROCKET[6] (Random Convolutional Kernel Transform) represents a paradigm shift: a large
bank (k = 10,000) of randomly initialized 1D convolutional kernels with random lengths, random
dilations, biases, and padding is applied. For each kernel, two global summary statistics are
extracted: the maximum value and proportion of positive values (PPV). These 2 k features
are fed to a linear ridge regression classifier. Despite its simplicity, ROCKET achieves near
state-of-the-art accuracy at inference times orders of magnitude faster than ensemble methods.
MiniROCKET[7] further simplified and accelerated ROCKET by restricting kernels to
a small fixed set of values {−1, 2} and using only PPV, reducing feature extraction time by a
factor of 75 while retaining 99% of accuracy.

2.4.2 GCNs for Time Series
TD-GCN[9] constructs a dynamic adjacency matrix at each time step by computing pairwise
cosine similarity between channel feature vectors.TapNet[12] combines random projection
and multi-head attention with a prototype-based classification framework.
2.4.3 FDNet
FDNet [3] introduces a two-branch architecture. One branch extracts temporal features from
the multivariate input through convolutional layers. The second branch computes pairwise
differences between time series instances and encodes these through a separate convolutional
pathway, explicitly embedding inter-sample discriminability into the representation. Three
notable limitations characterise this design. The pairwise difference branch grows quadratically
with the number of instances, constraining its applicability to large benchmarks. Within a
single instance, channels are processed independently before the difference computation, so no
intra-instance structural dependency is modelled. Consequently, there is also no hierarchical
relational context propagated beyond the pairwise difference signal itself.
2.4.4 SAGoG
SAGoG [4] addresses inter-instance structure more comprehensively through a two-level hierar-
chical graph construction. At the intra-instance level, a channel graph is built for each recording,
with edge weights reflecting pairwise channel similarity measured by Pearson correlation or
DTW; a GCN operating on this graph produces channel-level embeddings that are pooled into a
single instance representationh (i). At the inter-instance level, the set of pooled representations
{h(i)}N
i=1 forms the node set of a higher-level instance graph, with edge weights derived from
DTW similarity between raw time series; a second GCN propagates contextual information
between similarly behaving instances.
SAGoG’s hierarchical design delivers substantially improved accuracy on several benchmarks.
However, the DTW-based inter-instance graph construction and the subsequent GCN pass
together scale as O(N 2T 2) per training step, rendering the approach impractical for benchmarks
with large N or long T . The intra-instance channel graph operates at a single spatial scale, so
inter-channel relationships at other granularities go unmodelled. Neither FDNet nor SAGoG
achieves the jointly optimised integration of multi-scale temporal encoding and computationally
efficient hierarchical graph reasoning that all research gaps motivating the present work require.

2.6 Synthesis: Research Gaps
Four primary research gaps emerge from the survey above. No published method tightly
integrates a dilated TCN with a multi-scale GCN in a single jointly optimised framework;
temporal and spatial encoders are almost always designed independently and merged through
fixed-weight concatenation. Where graph convolution is employed, existing approaches construct
a single-radius adjacency matrix, leaving inter-channel relationships at other spatial scales
unrepresented. The full N×N inter-instance graph in SAGoG is computationally prohibitive at
scale; a segment-level hierarchical decomposition would deliver comparable relational context at
substantially lower cost.

Problem Statement and Objectives
Architecture is an argument. When a model uses dilated convolutions, it is making a bet
that useful patterns in the data span multiple time scales. When it builds a channel graph, it
is asserting that the relationships between sensor channels are structured enough to support
message-passing. When it introduces a learnable adjacency matrix, it is acknowledging that the
right graph topology cannot be hand-specified and must be discovered from the training data.
None of these are arbitrary choices: each one embeds a hypothesis about the structure of the
problem. If that hypothesis is wrong, the architectural feature it motivates will fail to provide
the claimed benefit the model may still learn something useful, but for reasons unrelated to the
intended mechanism.
This chapter provides the formal grounding that makes those hypotheses precise. It begins
with a rigorous mathematical definition of the MTSC task, introducing the notation used through-
out the remainder of the report. That definition reveals the problem’s natural decomposition into
three coupled subproblems: modelling temporal dependency within channels, modelling spatial
dependency between channels at a given time, and modelling relational dependency between
instances in the dataset. For each subproblem, the chapter explains not only what it means
formally but also why straightforward solutions Pearson correlation as a proxy for inter-channel
structure, mean pooling as a graph readout, nearest-neighbour matching as an inter-instance
similarity measure fail to capture the full complexity that real benchmark data exhibits.
The chapter then identifies seven specific difficulties that practitioners encounter when
applying MTSC methods to the UEA benchmark collection: channel heterogeneity, nonlinear
and time-varying inter-channel correlations, over-fitting on small training sets, class imbalance,
the computational cost of graph-based operations as a function of N and T , the inflexibility of
fixed-weight feature fusion, and the difficulty of interpreting what a trained model has actually
learned. Each difficulty is grounded in one or more concrete datasets to keep the discussion
empirically anchored.
Finally, these difficulties are consolidated into a precise statement of the research gap
the combination of properties that no existing published method achieves simultaneously and
converted into four research objectives, each tied to a specific module of the ST-GoG framework
and each paired with a measurable success criterion.

4.1 Overview of the ST-GoG Framework
The ST-GoG framework is built around a three-tier representational hierarchy that processes
each multivariate time series at three complementary levels of abstraction. At theTemporal
Tier, fine-grained patterns are extracted from each channel by a Residual TCN with five dilated
causal convolutional blocks ( d∈ { 1, 2, 4, 8, 16}) and Squeeze-and-Excitation channel attention.
At theSpatial Tier, a multi-scale GCN with three parallel branches at radii r∈ { 1, 3, 5} and
learnable softmax fusion captures inter-channel correlations across local, medium, and global
neighbourhoods. At theRelational Tier, each instance is decomposed into temporal segments
by a hierarchical GoG module, which encodes within-segment structure via an inner GCN
and propagates cross-segment context via an outer GCN. All three tiers are jointly optimised
end-to-end, with a learnable four-pathway fusion head that adaptively weights the contribution
of each tier to the final classification decision.

4.5 Design Considerations and Trade-offs
4.5.1 Chain Adjacency vs. Cosine Similarity
Chain adjacency encodes structural temporal proximity rather than instance-specific feature
similarity, eliminating the O(C2) pairwise construction cost and replacing it with a fixed sparse
structure of cost O(C·r ). The cached adjacency is reused across all instances of the same
dataset, further reducing overhead. The trade-off is that chain adjacency captures topological
structure rather than learned data-driven correlations.
4.5.2 Adaptive Segment Count for GoG
The adaptive segmentation ( S∈ { 4, 6, 8, 10} based on T ) ensures that each segment has
a minimum temporal length of approximately 10 time steps, maintaining meaningful within-
segment patterns for the inner GCN. For very long sequences (e.g., EigenWorms withT = 17,984),
the 10-segment split still creates large inner graphs that can be computationally intensive.
4.5.3 Learnable vs. Fixed Pathway Weights
Both the multi-scale GCN fusion weights (w scale) and the pathway weights (wpath) are learnable
parameters initialized to equal values (1 /3 and 1 /4 respectively) and optimized jointly with all
other parameters by gradient descent. Post-training inspection of these learned weights reveals
which spatial scales and which feature pathways are most discriminative for each dataset.

5.2 Baseline Model Implementation
5.2.1 Overview
The baseline model implements the minimal viable TCN–GCN–GoG pipeline:
Input→TCN→GCN→GoG→Classifier.
Several deliberate design choices shape the baseline. When the channel count exceeds the
sequence length ( C > T ), time steps rather than channels serve as graph nodes, ensuring the
adjacency matrix remains tractable. The adjacency itself encodes temporal proximity at radius
3 rather than data-driven pairwise similarity, which keeps construction cost at O(C·r ) and
allows reuse across all instances. The spatial encoder is a two-layer GCN with 64 hidden units
and ReLU nonlinearity, kept lightweight to isolate the contribution of the GoG head. Node
embeddings are finally mean-pooled across the graph to produce a fixed-length instance vector.

5.10 Limitations and Future Improvements
The current implementation carries five limitations. First, the 10-segment GoG on EigenWorms
(T = 17,984) creates inner GCN graphs of roughly 1800 nodes each, making per-epoch computa-
tion prohibitive; strided pre-downsampling before the GoG module would mitigate this. Second,
on FaceDetection (N = 5890), per-instance graph construction is a bottleneck that batch-level
graph sharing could resolve. Third, chain adjacency encodes topological proximity rather than
data-driven correlations; supplementing with a lightweight cosine-similarity component would
improve performance on datasets where cross-channel relationships are class-discriminative but
not topologically ordered. Fourth, replacing GoG mean pooling with an attention-weighted
readout would allow selective emphasis on the most discriminative temporal segments. Fifth,
future evaluations should include F1-scores, per-class confusion matrices, and GFLOPs profiling
alongside top-1 accuracy.

6
Conclusions and Future Scope
Accuracy on benchmark test sets is not the only measure of a research contribution. A model
that achieves strong performance but requires impractical computation, or whose behaviour
depends heavily on dataset-specific hyperparameter search, is a less useful contribution than
one that generalises reliably at reasonable cost. The ST-GoG framework was evaluated on both
dimensions.
Across 22 UEA datasets, the improved ST-GoG architecture recorded the highest mean
test accuracy (0.743) and achieved the most individual-dataset wins relative to any single
comparison method, while keeping computational cost well below that of SAGoG’s full pairwise
instance graph. The advantage was most pronounced on datasets with limited training data
such as AtrialFibrillation (15 training instances) and on datasets with structured inter-channel
dependencies, including Libras, NATOPS, FingerMovements, and ArticularyWordRecognition,
where the combination of multi-scale graph reasoning and data augmentation proved most
valuable.

6.1 Summary of Contributions
This thesis presented theSpatiotemporal Graph-of-Graphs (ST-GoG) Framework
through four cumulative contributions. Thebaseline TCN–GCN–GoG modelestablished
a clean, modular pipeline using chain adjacency, confirming that temporal, spatial, and re-
lational encoders can be integrated in a single end-to-end trainable system. Theimproved
ST-GoG architecturebuilt on that foundation by adding a five-layer residual TCN with SE
attention, a multi-scale GCN operating at three radii ( r∈ { 1, 3, 5}) with learnable fusion, an
adaptive segment-based hierarchical GoG, and a four-pathway learnable softmax fusion head a
combination that yields the best average accuracy and ten individual dataset wins across the
22-dataset benchmark. Theadvanced training packageMixUp ( α = 0.2), stochastic depth
with exponential decay, cosine annealing with warm restarts, label smoothing, and seven-pass
TTA delivered the largest gains on small and imbalanced datasets where the risk of overfitting is
greatest. Finally, thecomprehensive empirical studyproduced per-dataset accuracy tables,
win/tie/loss tallies, average rank scores, and a Wilcoxon-validated critical-difference diagram,
providing a thorough and statistically grounded comparison against SAGoG, ROCKET, and the
baseline.
6.2 Key Findings
Several consistent empirical patterns emerged from the 22-dataset evaluation. The multi-scale
GCN operating at three radii with learnable fusion outperformed the single-radius baseline
on datasets with multi-scale temporal dependencies, confirming that spatial modelling at a
single fixed scale leaves discriminative inter-channel signals unexploited. SE attention provided
consistent but modest gains, most pronounced on datasets where a small subset of temporal
feature maps carries disproportionate class-discriminative information. Inspection of learned
pathway weights revealed that GoG-augmented streams collectively captured the majority
of fusion weight on most datasets, validating the design decision of hierarchical segment-level
reasoning over simple global pooling. Despite these advantages, ROCKET remained the strongest
competitor on datasets with strong periodic structure or very long sequences, where its random-
kernel diversity efficiently extracts discriminative statistics without the computational overhead
of graph construction.

safety-critical applications.
6.3.6 Pre-Training and Transfer Learning
Large-scale pre-training via masked segment prediction predicting the embedding of a masked
temporal segment from surrounding segments could significantly improve performance on ex-
tremely small datasets (AF: 15 training instances, SWJ: 12 instances).
6.3.7 Real-Time and Edge Deployment
Optimizing ST-GoG for edge devices through knowledge distillation, INT8 quantization, and
pruning of the multi-scale GCN to a sparser single-scale GCN at inference time would enable
deployment in latency-constrained environments such as real-time arrhythmia monitoring.

A.1 Dataset Descriptions by Domain
The UEA Multivariate Time Series Archive [10] contains datasets from diverse domains. The
Motion and Activity Recognitiongroup (AWR, HW, LI, NA, RS, SWJ, UGL) comprises
multi-sensor inertial and position recordings in which discriminative patterns reside in the phase
and relative dynamics between channels. TheMedical and Neurologicalgroup (AF, EP,
HB, SCP1, SCP2, HMD, FM, FD, MI) covers electrocardiographic and electroencephalographic
recordings characterised by small training cohorts and clinically meaningful inter-class distinctions.
TheSensor Networks and Trafficgroup is represented principally by PEMS-SF, which
aggregates readings from 267 road-traffic-flow sensors over 144 time steps and exhibits strong
daily periodicity. TheAstronomy and Othergroup includes LSST, whose six-channel
photometric light curves capture stellar variability, as well as DuckDuckGeese, which encodes
bird wing-motion dynamics, and EigenWorms, which represents nematode body-shape evolution
via six eigenworm coefficients sampled at 17,984 time steps.
